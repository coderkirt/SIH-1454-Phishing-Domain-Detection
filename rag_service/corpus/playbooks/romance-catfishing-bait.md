## How the scam works

The fraudster adopts a fictitious, attractive persona on social media, matrimony apps, or dating platforms,
often claiming to be a military officer deployed overseas, an engineer on an offshore oil rig, or an international
businessperson. Over several weeks or months of daily communication, the fraudster builds intense emotional intimacy
and trust.

Eventually, the scammer introduces an urgent crisis: they sent an expensive gift or jewelry package that is held
at customs requiring clearance duties, or they suffered a sudden medical emergency abroad and cannot access their
accounts. The victim is persuaded to send money via wire transfer, cryptocurrency, or gift cards to save their partner.

## Common message patterns

- "My love, I dispatched a special package with gold and watches for you, but airport customs is holding it.
   Please pay the courier agent Rs 45,000 to clear the duty; I will repay you tenfold when I arrive."
- "I was involved in an accident abroad and my bank card is frozen. I urgently need $1,500 to pay the hospital."

These schemes exploit emotional vulnerability, isolation, and romantic commitment to overcome rational caution.

## Warning signs for analysts

- The individual always creates excuses to avoid video calls or in-person meetings (e.g., poor satellite signal).
- The relationship escalates rapidly with declarations of love and promises of marriage within days of meeting online.
- Sudden demands for financial assistance routed to third-party bank accounts, money mules, or crypto addresses.
- Instructions to keep the payments strictly confidential and hidden from family members or bank tellers.

## Recommended user guidance

Never send money, share bank details, or pay customs duties on behalf of someone you have only met online.
Perform a reverse image search on the individual's profile pictures to check if they belong to a model or stolen
public profile. Consult trusted family members or friends before making any financial transfers to online contacts.
